Act I — The proof it can work

If it worked there, why hasn't it worked in America?

The blueprint exists. We've seen it work.

In April 1996, a gunman killed 35 people at a tourist site in Port Arthur, Tasmania. Twelve days later, Australia passed sweeping national gun reform — banning semi-automatic weapons, launching a mandatory buyback of over 650,000 newly prohibited firearms (643,726 purchased by the federal government at market value under the 1996–97 buyback, with more than 700,000 guns removed in total from a population of about 12 million adults), and introducing uniform gun registration across every state.1

The results were stark. In the 18 years before Port Arthur, Australia experienced 13 mass shootings (defined in that study as incidents with five or more firearm-related homicides by one or two perpetrators in a civilian setting); in the 10.5 years that followed, there were zero. The rate of total firearm deaths, which had already been declining, accelerated — the estimated annual downward trend roughly doubled after the laws (for example, total firearm deaths: about 3% per year before vs about 6% after; p = 0.03). Firearm suicides fell faster still (p = 0.007), with no evidence in that analysis that people simply switched to other methods for homicide or suicide.1

Australia didn't eliminate guns. It didn't eliminate crime. But it made a national decision, and the data shows it worked.

So the question is simple: if it worked there, why hasn't it worked in America?

Act II — The country that couldn't

159 shootings. 40 states. No national reform.

Australia had 13 mass shootings in the 18 years before Port Arthur. The United States has had 159 in the 44 years since 1982 — killing at least 1,186 people, across 40 states, from Hawaii to Maine.2 The frequency has not held steady. In the 1980s the country averaged two or three a year. By the late 2010s it was averaging ten or more. The trend runs in one direction.

But mass shootings, as visible as they are, represent only a fraction of the toll. In 2022 alone, roughly 48,000 Americans died from firearms — homicides, suicides, and accidents combined — about 132 people every single day.3 Firearms are the leading cause of death for Americans between the ages of one and 44, surpassing car accidents, drug overdoses, and cancer in that age group.3 Australia's firearm death rate in 2018 was approximately 0.88 per 100,000 people. The United States rate that same year was more than fifteen times higher.

The United States has not been entirely inert. Congress has passed gun legislation. It just hasn't been enough, and what was passed has often been allowed to lapse.

In 1993, the Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act introduced federal background checks for firearm purchases — a genuine step that closed a real gap. A year later, the Federal Assault Weapons Ban prohibited the manufacture and sale of certain semi-automatic weapons and large-capacity magazines. Both measures were real. The assault weapons ban tracked closely with a period of relative stability in mass shooting frequency.

Then, in 2004, Congress allowed the assault weapons ban to expire without renewal. It has never been reinstated. In the years that follow, the frequency of mass shootings accelerates markedly. In 2022 — after Sandy Hook, after Parkland, after Uvalde — Congress passed the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act, the most significant federal gun legislation in nearly 30 years. It was a genuine step. The trend has not yet bent.

One argument you hear often is that gun violence in America is really a problem of particular places — specific cities, specific states, specific demographics. The geography of mass shootings does not support that. Shootings have occurred in 40 states. California leads with 26 incidents, Texas has 15, Florida 13 — but Montana, Iowa, Hawaii, and Rhode Island are on the map too.2 Workplaces, schools, places of worship, military bases, movie theaters, nightclubs, shopping malls. No geography has been spared. This is not a regional problem that regional policy can solve.

Act III — New York's experiment

If strict local laws were the answer, New York would be proof.

America has not reformed nationally. But it has tried locally, and nowhere has tried harder than New York City. The state has long had some of the strictest gun laws in the country: the Sullivan Act of 1911 required a permit for handgun possession a full century before most states even discussed it. After the Sandy Hook massacre in 2012, New York passed the SAFE Act in a matter of weeks — banning a long list of semi-automatic weapons, capping magazine sizes, and requiring universal background checks.

So if the argument is that strict local gun laws can solve this, New York City should be the proof. Twenty years of police records let us test that claim. The data — every shooting incident the NYPD has recorded since 2006, more than 24,000 of them4 — tells a story that is at once encouraging, infuriating, and unfinished.

The shape of two decades.

Every year since 2006, the NYPD has logged each shooting incident in the city. Plotted year by year, the line falls, then it collapses, then it spikes — and then it begins to fall again.

From 2006 to 2019: shootings cut in half.

1,566 → 777 incidents per year over thirteen years. A 50% decline. Whatever combination of policing, policy, demographics, and economic conditions drove that drop, it was real, sustained, and enormous in human terms.

Then, in a single year, it almost doubled.

2020. Shootings jumped from 777 to 1,532. A decade of progress erased in one summer. Whatever you believe caused it — COVID, the police pullback after the 2020 protests, economic collapse, the largest spike in firearm sales in U.S. history — the line on this chart does not care about explanations. It just spikes.

Here are the laws and rulings most often credited or blamed for New York's gun-violence trajectory. None of them produces a clean inflection in the data. Reform is slow, and noisy.

One number that doesn't move: lethality.

The dashed line is the share of shooting victims who die from their wounds. Year after year — through the long decline, through the 2020 spike, through the partial recovery — it sits between 15% and 18%. Volume changes; the rate at which shootings kill does not.

3.2 — Where it happens

Gun violence in NYC has a ZIP code.

The citywide trend obscures something important: shootings are not spread evenly across New York. They are concentrated, sharply, in a handful of neighborhoods.

All shootings, 2006–2024
Heatmap of every NYPD-recorded shooting incident

Every shooting, on one map.

More than 24,000 incidents over nineteen years. The hotspots are not subtle: a thick band running through northern Brooklyn, a cluster covering most of the Bronx, scattered density in northern Manhattan and southeast Queens. Wide stretches of the city — Staten Island, much of Manhattan, much of Queens — sit nearly empty.

Two boroughs carry most of the weight.

Brooklyn (40%) and the Bronx (29%) account for nearly seven in ten NYC shootings. That is not because they are biggest — Queens has roughly the same population as Brooklyn — it is because the same conditions that produce gun violence (concentrated poverty, segregated housing, under-investment) are concentrated in the same neighborhoods.

Three precincts. One small piece of Brooklyn.

Precincts 75, 73, and 67 — East New York, Brownsville, East Flatbush — together cover a few square miles. They alone account for a strikingly disproportionate share of the entire city's shootings. The same neighborhoods that were the most dangerous in 2006 are still the most dangerous today.

2019: the floor.

By 2019, the long decline had thinned the map dramatically. Even the historic hotspots looked manageable. This was the safest the city had been in living memory.

2020: the surge didn't hit equally.

One year later, the same map fills back in — but unevenly. The increase concentrates in the same neighborhoods that had historically borne the worst of it. The places already hit hardest paid the highest price for whatever 2020 was.

3.3 — Who bears the cost

Behind every dot on the map is a person.

Almost 29,000 victims since 2006. The demographic profile is remarkably narrow.

Loading victim records…

Mostly young.

Over 82% of NYC shooting victims are between 18 and 44. The single largest group is 25–44; the second is 18–24. Older victims are rare; victims over 65 are rarer still.

And too often, very young.

2,989 victims — about one in ten — were under 18 when they were shot. Children are not collateral; they are roughly a tenth of the entire dataset.

Almost entirely men.

91% male. Gun violence in NYC is, in the aggregate, a phenomenon that happens to men. Female victims exist and matter, but the volume is overwhelmingly male.

Lethality, by age.

About 1 in 6 of all shooting victims die. That headline rate is roughly stable across age groups, but it climbs sharply for the oldest victims — older bodies survive gunshot wounds less often.

We are deliberately not leading with race here. The racial distribution of victims tracks the geography of where shootings happen, which in turn reflects decades of housing policy, economic inequality, and policing patterns. Treating it as a stand-alone cause would invert the actual chain of cause and effect.

3.4 — When it happens

Gun violence runs on a clock and a calendar.

Aggregate twenty years of incidents by hour and day. A shape emerges, and it has not changed.

168 cells, twenty years of incidents.

Each cell is one hour of one day of the week, summed across every year from 2006 to 2024. Darker means more shootings. The shape is unmistakable.

Saturday night. Sunday night. Late.

The cluster glowing at the bottom-left and bottom-right is the weekend late-night peak — roughly 10pm Friday through 4am Sunday. Sunday alone records nearly 4,700 shootings. The early-morning hours of weekdays — your daily commute, the school run — are by far the safest.

Now switch to the calendar.

By month, July is the peak: 2,778 incidents cumulatively, more than double February's 1,254. Heat, daylight hours, time spent outside — gun violence rises with the temperature, every year, like a physical law.

3.5 — What the data can't tell us

Be honest about the limits.

The line in section 3.1 cannot prove that any specific law caused — or failed to cause — the trend you see. The 2013 SAFE Act and the curtailment of stop-and-frisk happened in the same year as part of a longer decline that was already under way. The 2020 spike has at least four plausible causes, and isolating any one of them is beyond what this data can support.

About 39% of incidents in this dataset have no identified shooter at all, which means any analysis of perpetrators starts from a base that is fundamentally incomplete. The data also tells us nothing about where the guns themselves came from — that would require ATF firearm-trace data New York doesn't control. And the dataset captures reported shootings; underreporting, especially of non-fatal incidents, is plausible.

With those caveats sitting on the table, the rest is yours.

The blueprint exists.

Australia made a national decision in 1996 and the data shows it worked. America has never been able to make the same decision, and every year roughly 48,000 of its citizens die from firearms. New York City did the most that any single city can do — wrote and enforced some of the strictest gun laws in the country — and the data shows what local reform can buy: a real, measurable, durable cut in shootings, vulnerable to a single bad year, concentrated against the same handful of neighborhoods, unable to control where the guns come from.

The blueprint exists. The evidence is here. What is missing is not data.
