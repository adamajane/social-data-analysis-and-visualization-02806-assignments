The Uneven Decline of Shootings in New York City

New York City is experiencing one of the safest periods for gun violence in its recent history. According to NYPD shooting records, incidents have fallen to their lowest level in the public dataset.

Across twenty years of records, shootings have not been evenly distributed across neighborhoods, time, or people. Some communities saw steep declines, while others remained persistent hotspots. The victims and offenders recorded in the data reveal patterns shaped by age, gender, race, geography, and circumstance.

This project explores those patterns through NYPD shooting incident, victim, and offender data from NYC Open Data 1. It begins with five guided chapters of charts and maps, then opens into an interactive section where readers can explore the data themselves.

Chapter 1 The long decline

The arc of the city and its boroughs.

New York City shootings have gradually declined in the last 20 years and in the year 2025 shootings were at an all-time low with only 688 shootings 1. There was a hiccup during the COVID-19 pandemic when shootings rose to previous highs, one reason for that is that during the crisis Americans felt they needed to protect themselves and flocked to the gun store, and in 2020 there was a 64% increase in gun sales compared to 2019, but has since then gotten back on track and continues to see record lows to this day 2.

There are multiple explanations for the decline of shootings in NYC and one of them is that gun laws are stricter in NYC than in other places in the USA, and throughout the years, necessary changes have been made in order to try and keep shootings under control, such as passing gun control legislation like the NY SAFE Act, which among other things enhanced background checks and banned assault weapons3.

Chapter 2 Where it happens

The Bronx and Brooklyn contain the most shootings.

Although the decline of shootings is happening in all New York City boroughs, the Bronx and Brooklyn do contain the most shootings. There is a multitude of factors that contribute to this. First of all Brooklyn is the most populated borough in NYC, so it having the most shootings is not a fair comparison 4 . The Bronx and Brooklyn are also the poorest boroughs in NYC with the median income of people that live in the Bronx being 47,034 dollars (~300,000 DKK) a year and 28% of people living there, live in poverty 4.

Even though gun laws are relatively strict in NYC, that does not stop a stream of illegal firearms coming in from elsewhere, and a 2020 study revealed that about 61.5% of recovered guns were found in Brooklyn and the Bronx 5. Further explaining why shootings seem to concentrate more in those two boroughs.

Chapter 3 Who it happens to

Behind every dot on the map is a person.

Across two decades of NYPD records, nearly 29,000 individuals have been shot in New York City1. They are not a cross-section of the city. The burden falls overwhelmingly on young men, and on the same small slice of geography we just explored3.

Knowing who is shot also tells us something about who survives. Older victims are far less likely to walk away from a gunshot wound, and the alleged shooters NYPD does identify are even younger than the victims themselves, most often teenagers and young adults.

Nearly 3,000 victims since 2006 have been under 18, about one in ten. Gun violence in New York reaches deep into childhood.

Almost entirely men.

Lethality rises with age.

Roughly one in six victims dies from their wounds. But age matters: a 65-year-old shot in New York is far less likely to walk out of the hospital than a 25-year-old.

Chapter 4 When it happens

Gun violence runs on a clock and a calendar.

Shootings in New York don't happen at random across the calendar. They follow a weekly and seasonal rhythm, concentrated at night, on weekends, on holidays, and in summer. A study of six major U.S. cities found the same pattern showing up almost everywhere it was measured7.

What might tie those hours to those wounds? Drinking is one well documented connector. A systematic review of four decades of research found that more than a third of firearm-violence decedents had consumed alcohol before their deaths, and that alcohol outlets are statistically associated with elevated rates of firearm assault8. Heat is another. A 2025 study of 44 U.S. cities found violent crime rising measurably as daily temperatures climb from cold to hot, with the strongest effects in cities with less greenspace and lower air-conditioning access9. Laid against NYC's late-night, weekend, and summer clusters, the picture begins to make sense.

Every hour of the week.

Each cell is one hour of one weekday. The darker, the more shootings. Routine workdays stay pale; the late-night blocks light up.

Friday and Saturday nights.

The cluster is hard to miss. Shootings concentrate from late Friday through Sunday morning. Counting the hours just after midnight as part of Sunday, Sunday alone accounts for roughly 4,700 incidents, more than any other day. Nightlife, weekends, and alcohol overlap heavily in this band8.

And then the summer surge.

July counts 2,778 incidents. More than double February's 1,254. Warm weather pulls people outdoors and stretches social gatherings later, and hotter days themselves raise violent-crime risk across U.S. cities9. The same weekend and late-night dynamics compound across the summer months7.

Chapter 5 Fatal vs. non-fatal

Incidents are not all equally lethal.

The city counts incidents; each can injure more than one person. Fatality is not the same as “how bad the year felt”, ED-based injury surveillance shows nonfatal gun wounds greatly outnumber deaths nationally, though no dataset captures every wound6.

What the data cannot prove

Limits sit beside every chart.

Nothing in Chapter 1's trend lines proves that any specific law caused or failed to cause the pattern you see. The 2013 SAFE Act and the curtailment of stop-and-frisk landed in the same year as a decline that was already under way. The 2020 spike has competing explanations; this notebook shows correlations on a map, not causes in a lab.

About 39% of incidents in this dataset have no identified shooter at all, which means any analysis of perpetrators starts from a base that is fundamentally incomplete. The data also tells us nothing about where the guns themselves came from, that would require ATF firearm-trace data New York doesn't control. And the dataset captures reported shootings; underreporting, especially of non-fatal incidents, is plausible.

Conclusion

Patterns are clear. Causes are not.

Across two decades of NYPD records, four findings hold up across every slice of the data. Shootings in New York City fell substantially from 2006 to 2019 before the 2020 surge erased much of that progress, and the city has only recently returned to its old floor. The geography is remarkably stable: Brooklyn and the Bronx, and a small cluster of precincts inside them, carry the bulk of the burden in every year on file. The people shot are overwhelmingly young men, and roughly one in six victims dies of their wounds, with the odds of survival falling as age rises. And the timing follows a consistent rhythm, late weekend nights and the summer months, the hours when nightlife, heat, and alcohol overlap.

Those are the patterns the data shows confidently. It is far quieter on why they exist. We can chart the 2020 surge but cannot isolate whether COVID lockdowns, a pullback in proactive policing, economic stress, or social unrest contributed most. We can name landmark policy moments, the 2013 SAFE Act, the 2022 Bruen ruling, the 2023 Concealed Carry Improvement Act, but the trend line was already moving before each, and a single city's incident file cannot causally attribute a curve to a statute. About 39% of incidents have no identified offender, so any picture of who pulls the trigger is structurally incomplete, and nothing in this dataset says where the guns themselves were bought or trafficked from.

The biggest blind spot is the wider structure that NYPD records do not capture at all. The neighborhoods that dominate the maps, East New York, Brownsville, East Flatbush, are also among the city's poorest, with lower median incomes and lower educational attainment than Manhattan or much of Queens. Decades of housing policy, school funding, employment access, and concentrated disinvestment leave fingerprints on a shooting map even though they appear nowhere in the file. The same is true of forces that sit further upstream, state and federal gun policy, the porous flow of firearms across state lines, cultural and political attitudes toward guns and policing, and what gets reported in the first place. New York's gun violence is, in this sense, a downstream symptom of much larger pressures that a city's own crime records were never built to measure.

What the data does say plainly is that the burden falls overwhelmingly on a small set of neighborhoods and a small set of people. That part is not in dispute. What the city, the state, and the country choose to do about it sits outside this dataset.
